## Overview

GradeX is a SaaS platform that centralises school operations for teachers, students, parents and administrators: attendance, grading, communication, inventory requests and staff workflows.

I owned the teacher-facing experience: the dashboards, tools and flows teachers use to manage classes, create and grade assessments, and communicate. **The platform was built from zero**, with no existing UI, design system or visual foundation. I worked alongside a lead designer and one other module owner, and we aligned through weekly syncs so the teacher, admin and parent experiences felt like one product.

This case study covers how I designed for dense institutional data while keeping the product usable for teachers with no technical background, across 50+ screens, two teacher roles and a six-month production cycle.

## The Brief & Research

The project started with a dense PRD from the product owner, covering multiple dashboards, digital grading, lesson management, inventory requests and leave applications.

To ground the work, we ran a competitive analysis of Schoology, Alma and Edmodo. The same issues kept appearing: cluttered dashboards, weak navigation, and **interfaces that assumed too much technical literacy** from users who are teachers first.

Combining those insights with the stakeholder documentation, I defined two personas for the teacher side:

* Main teachers: full access to dashboards, grading tools, analytics and administrative features.
* Substitute teachers: a reduced experience focused on attendance and lesson schedules.

That set the central constraint: the platform had to be fully capable across its feature set without ever becoming a burden for **someone whose actual job was teaching, not navigating software**.

## Framing the Problem

**The core issue with designing such a platform was balance. The product owner wanted depth, data density and broad feature coverage; teachers needed clarity, speed and as little cognitive load as possible. My job was to serve both.**

I framed the work around four questions:

1. Dashboard density: how do you show a lot of information without overwhelming someone who’s already context-switching between thirty students?
2. Information hierarchy: what does a teacher need to see immediately, and what can sit one level deeper?
3. Functional parity: how do you keep flows and navigation consistent across roles with different permissions, without either role feeling like a degraded version of the other?
4. Scalability: how do you make every layout decision hold across desktop, tablet and mobile?

## Design Approach

I structured ideation around three pillars, each aimed at a failure mode from the competitive analysis:

1. Information architecture: the teacher module alone covered 50+ unique screens. I grouped related tasks to cut redundancy and **applied progressive disclosure throughout**: dashboards surface attendance, active lessons and performance trends at a glance, with deeper analytics one click away. Dashboard cards work as quick-view summaries, so routine checks never require a full detail view.
2. Intentional UX writing: most teachers had no technical background, so every label, prompt, error state and microcopy string was written (sometimes with help from AI models) **to guide rather than instruct**, in plain language. Error states tell users what happened and what to do next, not just that something went wrong.
3. Cross-module consistency: different designers owned different roles, but a teacher moving into a shared flow should never land somewhere that feels like a different product. \
   We built a lightweight internal design system covering layout grids, spacing and interaction logic, and used the weekly syncs to catch divergence early. The result was a platform where every section behaved like part of the same product family, whoever designed it.

## The Design

With the approach settled, I built high-fidelity prototypes for the full teacher experience: 50+ screens across both teacher roles. The core areas:

* Home dashboard: today’s attendance, active lessons and student analytics in one view, structured to **answer a teacher’s first three questions of the day without any navigation**.
* Class management: student lists, grading tools and performance insights in one consolidated view, reducing context switches between related tasks.
* Assignments and exams: setup, submission tracking and review, with clear state indicators so teachers always know what needs action.
* Inventory requests: a streamlined supply request, two steps from dashboard to submission.
* Leave applications: a request and approval flow built for infrequent use, so it had to be self-explanatory on first contact.

## Usability Testing & Refinement

Once the teacher designs were complete, the full team ran remote moderated usability tests with teachers, substitute teachers, students, parents and administrators across 20+ schools. Tasks mirrored real workflows: marking attendance, submitting assignments, approving leave, managing supplies and tracking lessons.

The response was strong: teachers found the dashboards clear and easy to navigate. One issue came up consistently: **fatigue when scanning dense data tables** during long sessions. Three targeted fixes addressed it:

* Alternating row colours for visual separation without adding structural weight.
* More vertical spacing to relieve the compressed feel of high-density views.
* A Quick Actions panel on the dashboard that cut the five most frequent teacher tasks from 3 clicks to 1.

These fixes went into the design system and were implemented with the developers to keep handoff accurate.

## Results & Impact

<Figure images={[{"src":"/src/assets/case-studies/002/stats-results-and-impact.webp","alt":"Four stat tiles: 6 months from brief to launch, 20+ schools in the pilot cohort, 95% approval in usability testing, 50+ screens designed for the teacher module"}]} layout="stack" frame="border" />

GradeX finished its six-month development cycle and launched. In the separate moderated sessions I ran with 12 teachers from prospective schools that had shown interest in GradeX (recruited by the product owner), **core teacher workflows reached a ~95% task completion rate**. Across the wider pilot cohort, teachers found the dashboards clear and intuitive.

The three table refinements were validated in follow-up test rounds before handoff, confirming they solved the fatigue issue without adding navigation complexity.

The teacher experience **shipped production-ready**, with a complete developer handoff file covering component specs, interaction states and responsive behaviour.
